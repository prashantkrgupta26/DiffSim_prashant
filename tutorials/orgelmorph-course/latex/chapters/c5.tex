\chapter{Two dimensions versus three}
\label{ch:c5}
\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} Going from 2-D to 3-D is not a small constant factor.
Every refinement multiplies the degrees of freedom by $4$ in 2-D but $8$
in 3-D; the matrix grows denser per row for reasons rooted in the finite-
element \emph{connectivity} (not a finite-difference stencil); the full
memory footprint --- far more than the CSR values --- overruns the card
and the 32-bit index arithmetic; and, crucially, the \emph{physics} at
equal resolution is different. 2-D is not a cheap 3-D.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Learning objectives.} By the end you can: explain why dofs
scale as $2^{\dim}$ per refinement level ($\times4$ in 2-D, $\times8$ in
3-D); derive the sparsity count
$(2p+1)^{\dim}\times n_{\text{fields}}$ from element connectivity,
basis order, fields, and dimension, and show why a naive
``$3^{\dim}-1$'' finite-difference stencil undercounts it; produce a
complete device-memory accounting (not just the CSR values) with
\code{estimate\_capacity.py}; explain why int32 CSR indices are an
\emph{implementation choice} with a real overflow risk once
nnz$>2^{31}$, not a hardware limit; and show that 2-D is not a cheap
3-D at equal resolution (interfacial-area density, $S(q)$ wavelength,
and percolation differ).\\[2pt]
\textbf{Prerequisites.} Finite-element connectivity and CSR sparsity
patterns; Chapter~00 (environment green); Chapter~\ref{ch:c1} and
Chapter~\ref{ch:c4} (this track).\\[2pt]
\textbf{Expected cost.} Any CUDA GPU; the live 2-D/tiny-3-D runs use
well under 1\,GB device memory (an 8\,GB card is ample). Solver
\code{blockch} by default in \code{estimate\_capacity.py} (or
\code{splu}/\code{cudss}). The $128^3$ and $256^3$ memory figures below
are \emph{estimated} capacity-planning numbers, not runs: $128^3$ fits a
48\,GB card, $256^3$ does not and is never actually launched here.
Live-run cost is of the same order as physics P1's quick mode
($\approx75$\,s wall, RTX~6000~Ada, mostly kernel compile).
\end{keybox}

This concept measures the 2-D-vs-3-D growth live on small meshes,
accounts the memory completely, and shows that the morphology itself
changes with dimension. It is the capstone of the computational track:
it explains \emph{why} everything in Chapter~\ref{ch:c4} (the device
assembly path, the \texttt{blockch} solver, the matrix-free frontier)
exists.

\section{Where the sparsity really comes from}

Refine a mesh by one level and the number of elements multiplies by
$2^{\dim}$ --- $4$ in 2-D, $8$ in 3-D. That much is obvious. The density
of the matrix is subtler, and it is easy to state wrong.

A row of the Jacobian is a degree of freedom. Its nonzeros are one per
$(\text{coupled node})\times(\text{field})$, where two nodes couple iff
they share a finite \emph{element}. For a degree-$p$ structured mesh the
coupled nodes form a $(2p+1)^{\dim}$ block --- for $p=1$, $3^{\dim}$:
\emph{nine} nodes in 2-D, \emph{twenty-seven} in 3-D (the node itself
included). The mixed $(c,\mu)$ system carries $2$ fields, and within a
coupled node pair the $2\times2$ field block is dense, so
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\text{nnz}}{\text{dof}}
  \;=\; \underbrace{(2p+1)^{\dim}}_{\text{connectivity}\,\times\,\text{order}\,\times\,\dim}
        \;\times\; \underbrace{n_{\text{fields}}}_{\text{block}}
  \;=\; 18\ (\text{2-D}),\quad 54\ (\text{3-D}).
\end{equation}
%
So the density is set by five things: element \textbf{connectivity},
basis \textbf{order} $p$, number of \textbf{fields}, the field
\textbf{block} structure, and \textbf{dimension} (hanging-node
\textbf{constraints} add a few more couplings). A ``$3^{\dim}-1$
finite-difference stencil'' is the wrong mental model: it omits the node
itself and the field block, and undercounts. The \emph{measured} averages
(\CfiveNnzPerDofTwoD\ in 2-D, \CfiveNnzPerDofThreeD\ in 3-D) sit just below
the interior maxima $18$ and $54$ because boundary nodes have fewer
neighbours.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The compounding.} 2-D dofs grow $\times\CfiveDofGrowthTwoD$/level
and carry $\CfiveNnzPerDofTwoD$ nnz/dof; 3-D dofs grow
$\times\CfiveDofGrowthThreeD$/level and carry $\CfiveNnzPerDofThreeD$. Four
levels is $256\times$ the dofs in 2-D but $4096\times$ in 3-D --- and each
3-D dof is $\sim3\times$ denser. This is why a code that is instant in 2-D
needs a GPU, a device assembler, and a scalable solver in 3-D.
\end{keybox}

\section{Accounting the memory completely}

The CSR \emph{values} are only a fraction of what a real solve holds. The
\file{estimate\_capacity.py} CLI accounts every buffer: CSR values, column
indices \emph{and} row pointers, the device index mirror a resident
assembler keeps, the Newton solution/residual and the BDF history vectors,
the local-to-global connectivity map, quadrature/geometry tables, the
solver workspace (direct \emph{fill-in} for splu/cuDSS, or the extracted
preconditioner blocks for \texttt{blockch}), and the saved output snapshot.

\begin{runbox}
\textbf{Estimate before you launch.}
\begin{lstlisting}
python estimate_capacity.py --dim 3 --n 128 --solver blockch
python estimate_capacity.py --dim 3 --n 256              # exceeds 48 GB
python estimate_capacity.py --dim 2 --level 8 --solver splu --idx-bytes 8
\end{lstlisting}
\end{runbox}

At $128^3$ the total is $\sim\CfiveMemOneTwoEight\,$GB --- dominated by the
\emph{solver workspace}, not the matrix values --- and it fits a 48\,GB
card. At $256^3$ it is $\sim\CfiveMemTwoFiveSix\,$GB and does \emph{not}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:c5mem}); only a matrix-free or multi-GPU path remains. The
lesson is that ``will it fit?'' is answered by the \emph{complete}
footprint, and the dominant term is often the solver's, not the matrix's.

\paragraph{The int32 ceiling is an implementation choice.}
The CSR index arithmetic here is 32-bit, so nnz $>2^{31}\approx2.1\times
10^{9}$ overflows the slot arithmetic. That is a property of \emph{this
build}, not a law: an int64 index build lifts the wall (at $2\times$ the
index memory), and a distributed (multi-rank) or block-masked pattern
sidesteps it by never storing one monolithic CSR. The cited
$(M{=}3,K{=}2)$ films cross $2^{31}$, which is why the block-masked
pattern exists (it drops the structural zeros the superset stores).

\section{Physics at equal resolution: 2-D is not cheap 3-D}

The deepest reason to care about the cost of 3-D is that you cannot dodge
it by working in 2-D: at the \emph{same} resolution the two answer
\emph{different physical questions}. We quench the same spinodal blend in
2-D and 3-D at identical $h=\CfivePhysH$ and measure the morphology
(Fig.~\ref{fig:c5phys}):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item interfaces are \emph{curves} in 2-D but \emph{surfaces} in 3-D, so
        the interfacial-area density differs ($\CfiveAreaTwoD$ vs
        $\CfiveAreaThreeD$ in length$^{d-1}$ per unit volume);
  \item the characteristic $S(q)$ wavelength differs
        ($\CfiveLamTwoD$ vs $\CfiveLamThreeD$);
  \item connectivity differs in kind: a 3-D blend can be \emph{bicontinuous}
        (both phases percolate simultaneously), which is topologically
        impossible for two phases in 2-D. Percolation, coarsening exponents,
        and stratification under a substrate are all dimension-specific.
\end{itemize}
So a 2-D study is a legitimate, cheaper \emph{model problem} --- excellent
for convergence, BCs, solver behaviour --- but its \emph{morphology} is not
a thin slice of the 3-D answer. Report which question you are asking.

\paragraph{Domain and aspect ratio.}
Two more knobs the cost hides: a larger \emph{domain} at fixed $h$ scales
dofs as (box size)$^{\dim}$ --- linearly in area (2-D) but as volume
(3-D); and a thin-film \emph{aspect ratio} (wide $x,y$, thin $z$, e.g.
$64\times64\times16$) keeps 3-D tractable by shrinking the cheap direction,
which is exactly the \code{slab64} class on the ladder above. Choosing the
domain and aspect ratio is choosing where on the cost curve you sit.
