\chapter{Boundary conditions, numerically}
\label{ch:c2}
\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} The mixed Cahn--Hilliard system has \emph{two}
boundary conditions, not one --- and the \emph{way} each is imposed
decides what the simulation conserves and whether its matrix stays
symmetric. A no-flux (natural) boundary costs nothing and conserves mass
exactly; a Dirichlet boundary is imposed by editing rows of the linear
system and \emph{deliberately} breaks conservation; a weakly imposed
(penalty) boundary trades exactness for a symmetric matrix.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Learning objectives.} By the end you can: derive the two
boundary terms of the mixed $(c,\mu)$ weak form (the mass flux
$M\,\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n$ and the interfacial term
$\kappa\,\grad c\cdot\mathbf n$) and say which BCs make each vanish;
compare natural, wall-energy (wetting/Robin), Dirichlet, and
manufactured (MMS) conditions on whether they conserve mass; verify the
flux balance $d/dt\intv c\,dV=-\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf J\cdot
\mathbf n\,dS$ directly from a run; and contrast weak (penalty) versus
strong (row-replace / symmetric-elimination) imposition, including the
penalty parameter's exactness-vs-conditioning trade-off.\\[2pt]
\textbf{Prerequisites.} Integration by parts / the weak form, symmetric
vs.\ non-symmetric linear systems; Chapter~\ref{ch:c1} (this track's
convergence study) and Physics Chapter~\ref{ch:p3} (the physical wall
energy); Chapter~00 (environment green).\\[2pt]
\textbf{Expected cost.} Any CUDA GPU, 2-D, $<1$\,GB device memory (an
8\,GB card is ample); solver \code{splu} on the $(c,\mu)$ saddle (the
tiny weak-vs-strong demo is a dense NumPy solve, no GPU). \code{run.py}
completes in $\approx$30--60\,s, comparable to Physics P1's quick mode
($\approx75$\,s wall, measured, RTX~6000 Ada; mostly kernel compile);
expect the same first-run Warp-compile overhead.
\end{keybox}

This concept shows, with measured numbers, the full boundary structure of
the mixed system, the taxonomy of physical boundary conditions, how each

\section{Two boundary conditions live in the weak form}

The mixed system solves \emph{two} equations, so integrating each by
parts produces \emph{two} surface integrals. For the conserved transport
equation, multiply by a test function $w$ and integrate by parts:
%
\begin{equation}
  \intv w\,c_t\;+\;M\!\intv \grad w\cdot\grad\mu
  \;=\; M\!\oint_{\partial\Omega} w\,(\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n)\,dS .
  \label{eq:weakbc}
\end{equation}
%
For the chemical-potential equation $\mu = f'(c) - \kappa\grad^2 c$, the
same treatment of the $\grad^2 c$ term gives a \emph{second} surface
integral:
%
\begin{equation}
  \intv q\,\mu \;=\; \intv q\,f'(c) \;+\;\kappa\!\intv\grad q\cdot\grad c
  \;-\;\kappa\!\oint_{\partial\Omega} q\,(\grad c\cdot\mathbf n)\,dS .
  \label{eq:weakmu}
\end{equation}
%
So the boundary enters in \emph{two} places: the mass flux
$M\,\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n$ in \eqref{eq:weakbc}, and the interfacial
term $\kappa\,\grad c\cdot\mathbf n$ in \eqref{eq:weakmu}. A complete
boundary condition must say what happens to \emph{both}.

\paragraph{Natural (no-flux + no-wetting).}
Setting $\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n=0$ \emph{and}
$\grad c\cdot\mathbf n=0$ makes both surface terms vanish, and you impose
them by \emph{doing nothing} --- simply omit the integrals. This is why
they are called \emph{natural}. Physically: no mass crosses the wall
(zero flux) and the interface meets the wall at $90^\circ$ (no wetting
preference). Total mass $\intv c\,dV$ is then conserved to solver
tolerance; every spinodal run in Physics Chapter~\ref{ch:p1} is a natural
run, which is why its mass drift was machine epsilon.

\paragraph{Wall free energy (wetting / Robin).}
A real thin film often \emph{wets} its substrate: one component prefers
the wall. Adding a surface free energy $\oint g_s(c)\,dS$ to
\eqref{eq:F} makes the $\kappa\,\grad c\cdot\mathbf n$ term \emph{nonzero}
--- the condition becomes $\kappa\,\grad c\cdot\mathbf n + g_s'(c) = 0$, a
Robin-type wetting condition that still keeps $\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n=0$
(no mass crosses, so mass is \emph{still} conserved). It is genuine
physics but lives in the multiphase film brick, not this binary brick
(see \file{docs/dev/2026-07-13-m5-device-assembly.md}, the A2 wall term);
we name it here for completeness and use it in the device track.

\section{The taxonomy}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
condition & imposes & conserves mass? \\
\midrule
natural (no-flux) & $\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n=0$, $\grad c\cdot\mathbf n=0$ & yes (exactly) \\
wall energy (wetting) & $\kappa\grad c\cdot\mathbf n+g_s'(c)=0$ & yes \\
prescribed composition & $c = g$ (Dirichlet on $c$) & no (reservoir) \\
prescribed potential & $\mu = g$ (Dirichlet on $\mu$) & no \\
periodic & $c,\mu$ identified across faces & yes \\
manufactured (MMS) & $c=g_c$ \emph{and} $\mu=g_\mu$ & no (device) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Manufactured vs.\ physical Dirichlet.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:c1} pinned \emph{both} $c$ and $\mu$ on the boundary.
That is an \textbf{MMS device}, not a physical reservoir: it forces the
discrete solution to match a known field so the error is measurable. A
\emph{physical} composition-controlled contact pins only $c=g$; what $\mu$
should be there follows from the interior physics and is \emph{not} ours
to set. Pinning both over-determines the boundary --- perfectly fine for
manufacturing an exact solution, wrong as a model of a real contact. This
tutorial's ``Dirichlet'' runs pin $c=g$ (and, for the binary brick's
convenience, $\mu=0$); read that $\mu=0$ as a modelling shortcut, not a
law (Exercise).

\section{How a constraint is implemented}

A prescribed value $u_i = g$ is a linear-algebra edit of the assembled
Newton system $A\,\delta x = r$. There are three ways, and they differ in
what they do to the matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:c2matrix}), shown on a tiny
$-u''=0$ system whose exact solution is the straight line
$u=a+(b-a)x$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Row replacement.} Overwrite row $i$ with the identity
        row, $A_{i,:}\!\leftarrow e_i^\top$, $r_i\!\leftarrow g-x_i$. Fast
        and exact, but it \emph{breaks symmetry} (the column is left
        intact) --- measured \code{symmetric = }\CtwoRowReplaceSym. A
        non-symmetric matrix rules out a Cholesky/CG path.
  \item \textbf{Symmetric elimination.} Also zero column $i$, moving its
        known contribution to the right-hand side. Keeps the matrix
        symmetric-positive-definite (\code{symmetric =
        }\CtwoSymElimSym) at the cost of touching the RHS. This is what a
        Jacobian assembly should do so the tangent stays SPD.
  \item \textbf{Weak (penalty / Nitsche).} Add a large penalty to the
        diagonal, $A_{ii}\!\mathrel{+}=\beta$, $r_i\!\mathrel{+}=\beta g$.
        The value is imposed only \emph{approximately}, to
        $\mathcal O(1/\beta)$, but symmetry is preserved and no row is
        destroyed --- convenient on hanging-node or immersed boundaries
        where a clean row does not exist.
\end{enumerate}
On a hanging-node octree, a Dirichlet node that is also \emph{constrained}
(a hanging node interpolated from its parents) cannot simply have its row
replaced --- the constraint operator $T$ must be applied first, and the
boundary value distributed to the parent nodes. The brick's constraint
matrix $T$ handles this; the row edits above act in the \emph{reduced}
(free-node) space $A = T^\top K\,T$.

\section{The flux balance ties conservation to the wall}

Integrating the conserved dynamics over the whole domain and using the
divergence theorem gives an exact statement:
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dt}\intv c\,dV \;=\;
  M\!\oint_{\partial\Omega}\grad\mu\cdot\mathbf n\,dS
  \;=\; -\oint_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf J\cdot\mathbf n\,dS,
  \qquad \mathbf J=-M\grad\mu.
  \label{eq:fluxbal}
\end{equation}
%
The mass changes at \emph{exactly} the rate material crosses the wall. In
the discrete conserved scheme these two are the \emph{same} quantity, so
we measure the net flux directly as $dm/dt$ from the quadrature-mass
series (Fig.~\ref{fig:c2flux}). Under no-flux the boundary integral is
zero and $dm/dt = \CtwoNfFlux$ (machine precision --- mass exactly
conserved). Under Dirichlet the wall is a reservoir: the influx is
$\CtwoDirFluxEarly$ early and decays to $\CtwoDirFluxLate$ as the interior
equilibrates to the wall value and the reservoir stops pumping. Watching
the flux shut off is the measurable signature of \eqref{eq:fluxbal}.

\section{Code walk-through}

The study lives in \file{bc.py}, driving the same production brick.

\paragraph{Selecting the boundary.}
\begin{lstlisting}
# natural: pass nothing -- the default weak form is no-flux + no-wetting
st = CahnHilliardStepper(dm, M, kap, dt, order=1)
# Dirichlet: pass the boundary node indices + the data functions
st = CahnHilliardStepper(dm, M, kap, dt, order=1, dirichlet=bidx,
                         gc_fn=lambda x,t: wall, gm_fn=lambda x,t: 0)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{The three impositions (tiny system).}
\begin{lstlisting}
dirichlet_strong(K, b, i, g)                 # row replace (asymmetric)
dirichlet_strong(K, b, i, g, symmetric=True) # + column elim (SPD kept)
dirichlet_penalty(K, b, i, g, beta)          # weak, O(1/beta)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Flux balance and the test matrix.}

flux_balance("noflux");  flux_balance("dirichlet")  # dm/dt = net flux
bc_test_matrix()   # natural / +0.9 / -0.9 / 0.0 / mixed, one blend
\end{lstlisting}

device geometries (contacts on two sides, sealed on the others).}

\question{Under our Dirichlet runs the boundary $\mu$ is pinned to $0$.
Argue what $\mu$ at a composition-pinned contact \emph{should} be, and why
pinning both $c$ and $\mu$ is a modelling choice (an MMS device) rather
than a law. What would a $c$-only Dirichlet require of the solver?}

\question{Raise the penalty $\beta$ past $10^{6}$ in the weak-BC demo. The
