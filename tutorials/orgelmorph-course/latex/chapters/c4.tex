\chapter{The solver ecosystem}
\label{ch:c4}
\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} Every implicit step ends in a linear solve of the
\emph{indefinite} $(c,\mu)$ Cahn--Hilliard saddle, and the choice of
solver is a \emph{correctness} decision before it is a speed one. A
general-purpose GPU direct solver (cuDSS) \textbf{diverges} on the
polynomial CH saddle --- it does no pivoting, and the polynomial well is
indefinite in the spinodal band --- so the field blows up. The pivoted CPU
direct solver \code{splu} is safe at small scale; the CH-specific
\texttt{blockch} preconditioner, which splits the saddle into
symmetric-positive-definite sub-solves, is the choice at scale. \emph{Not}
cuDSS on the raw CH block.
\end{keybox}

This concept teaches how to choose, using a real marched divergence test
--- the honest way to check a solver on the CH saddle --- plus the live
2-D timing and the cited 3-D scaling tables.

\section{The real test: march it and look at the solution}

Each Newton iterate solves $A\,\delta = r$, where $A$ is the mixed
$(c,\mu)$ Cahn--Hilliard Jacobian --- a \emph{saddle}-structured,
\emph{indefinite} sparse matrix. The tempting shortcut is to solve one
captured system and check its residual. That shortcut \textbf{lies}: on
the polynomial saddle, cuDSS's residual at a single Newton iterate is
$\CfourCudssResid$ (as small as \code{splu}'s $\CfourSpluResid$), yet
\emph{marching} the real 20-step spinodal with cuDSS blows the field up.
A small residual does not imply a small error when the matrix is
ill-conditioned. So we measure the \emph{marched solution}
(Table~\ref{tab:c4diverge}, Fig.~\ref{fig:c4diverge}).

\section{Why: an indefinite saddle with no pivoting}

The polynomial well $f(c)=\tfrac14(c^2-1)^2$ has bulk curvature
$f''(c)=3c^2-1$, which is \emph{negative} across the spinodal band
$|c|<1/\sqrt3$ --- exactly the region the dynamics drives the field into.
There the $(c,\mu)$ block is genuinely \emph{indefinite}. cuDSS
factorizes without the partial pivoting an indefinite system needs, so its
solution is wrong, and the error compounds over the Newton and time
iterations until the field escapes. Flory--Huggins survives because its
entropic curvature $f''=A\!\left(\tfrac1c+\tfrac1{1-c}\right)\ge 4A$ stays
\emph{positive} and better-conditioned --- but you cannot count on that in
general, and the production default must be safe for the polynomial well.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The recipe this forces.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{small:} \code{splu} --- pivoted CPU direct, safe on the
        indefinite saddle.
  \item \textbf{at scale:} \texttt{blockch} / \code{blockch\_dev} --- an
        FGMRES around a block preconditioner that splits the saddle into
        \emph{SPD} sub-solves (mass, $W_1$, $W_2$), so no monolithic
        indefinite factorization is ever formed.
  \item \textbf{not} cuDSS on the raw CH block. (cuDSS is fine on the
        well-conditioned SPD sub-blocks a preconditioner factors, and on
        block-masked 3-D film patterns.)
\end{itemize}
The \code{--solver auto} default of \code{splu} for CH is correct.
\end{keybox}

\section{The 2-D timing (context only)}

With correctness settled, we still measure speed --- but note that for CH
the fastest 2-D solver is \emph{disqualified}. Timing the linear solve
(factorize $+$ solve, cold and warm, CUDA-synchronised median) shows cuDSS
overtaking \code{splu} as the 2-D problem grows past $\sim10^4$ dofs (at
$\CfourBigDofs$ dofs it is $\sim\CfourBigSpeedup\times$ faster;
Fig.~\ref{fig:c4cross}). But that speed is unusable on the CH saddle
because cuDSS returns a wrong answer there. The lesson: \emph{fastest} is
not \emph{correct}.

\section{The 3-D story, cited}

The 3-D numbers take minutes per step, so we cite the dev-note tables. At
scale the \emph{memory} of a direct factorization compounds the accuracy
problem: cuDSS's fill hits the 48\,GB card at $\sim\CfourCudssCeiling$
dofs (a $>16$\,min factorization that never returns). \code{blockch\_dev}
beats masked cuDSS $\CfourBlockchSpeedup\times$ on the slab64 solve and
keeps marching where cuDSS ceilings; a block-masked cuDSS pattern cuts the
fill ($17.7\to\CfourMaskedScall$ s/call) below the ceiling; AMGX adds
nothing at production $\Delta t$ (mass-dominated inners); and matrix-free
reaches $\CfourMatrixfreeDofs$ dofs on one card (Fig.~\ref{fig:c4cited}).

\section{Decision support, with a rationale}

Rather than a one-line \code{choose\_solver(dim, dofs)}, the tutorial's
\code{recommend\_solver} weighs dofs, nnz, block structure, precision,
memory, factorization reuse, tolerance, $\Delta t$, and hardware, and
returns a \emph{rationale} that begins with the correctness constraint:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
regime & dofs (order) & recommendation \\
\midrule
2-D, small        & $<2\times10^4$   & \code{splu} (pivoted; safe on saddle) \\
2-D, large        & $<5\times10^5$   & \code{splu} / \texttt{blockch} (\emph{not} cuDSS) \\
3-D, small        & $<5\times10^5$   & masked cuDSS / \code{blockch\_dev} \\
3-D, large        & up to $\sim1.5\times10^6$ & \code{blockch\_dev} \\
3-D, extreme      & $>$ int32 / memory & matrix-free (or multi-GPU) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Every recommendation names \emph{why} --- and never picks cuDSS on the raw
indefinite CH block.

\section{Code walk-through}

\paragraph{The real correctness test (marched, not one-shot).}
\begin{lstlisting}
march_divergence()      # marches 20 steps per (energy, solver); c.min/max
\end{lstlisting}
It marches the production stepper and reads the final field range --- the
only honest test on an indefinite saddle.

\paragraph{The trap, and the supported capture API.}
\begin{lstlisting}
solver_correctness()    # one-iterate residual: cuDSS looks fine (~1e-13)!
st = CahnHilliardStepper(..., capture_system=True)   # no monkeypatch
A, b = st.last_system
\end{lstlisting}
The one-iterate residual is captured through a supported stepper flag (not
a \code{solve\_linear} monkeypatch) --- and it \emph{lies}, which is the
point.

\section{Explore on your own}

\question{Reproduce the divergence: march the polynomial spinodal with
cuDSS for $5, 10, 15, 20$ steps and plot $\max|c|$ versus step. Is the
blow-up gradual or sudden, and what does that say about how the
unpivoted-factorization error compounds?}

\question{cuDSS survives Flory--Huggins because $f''\ge4A>0$. Lower the FH
regularization floor (push $c$ toward $0$ or $1$ so $f''$ spikes) and
re-march. Does cuDSS still survive, and at what conditioning does it
break?}

\question{The one-iterate residual for cuDSS was $\CfourCudssResid$ ---
tiny --- yet the march diverged. Construct the argument (condition number
$\times$ residual bounds the error) that explains how a small residual can
accompany a large error, and estimate the effective condition number
needed.}

\question{\texttt{blockch} splits the saddle into SPD sub-solves. Read the
design-laws in \file{src/diffsim/solvers/linsolve.py}: which naive block
preconditioners \emph{also} diverge on FH, and why does the two-factor
signed-$F$ form survive? Contrast that (a preconditioner failure) with the
cuDSS direct-solve failure here.}

\question{Using the cited cuDSS memory ceiling ($\sim\CfourCudssCeiling$
dofs on 48\,GB), estimate it on an 80\,GB A100 --- and note that even
where cuDSS \emph{fits} in 3-D, it is still the raw indefinite block, so
the masked pattern (SPD-friendlier) or \code{blockch\_dev} is what you
actually run.}
