\chapter{The solver ecosystem}
\label{ch:c4}
\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} There is no single best linear solver. A direct
factorization (cuDSS) is unbeatable in 2-D and up to a memory wall in
3-D; past that wall it dies, and a block preconditioner
(\texttt{blockch}) wins by an order of magnitude; past the point where
even the matrix will not fit, only a matrix-free method survives.
Choosing the solver is choosing where on that curve your problem sits.
\end{keybox}

This concept teaches how to choose, using a small live 2-D comparison
plus the measured 3-D scaling tables from the device-assembly and
\texttt{blockch} development notes.

\section{Three families of solver}

Each Newton iterate solves $A\,\delta = r$, where $A$ is the mixed
$(c,\mu)$ Cahn--Hilliard Jacobian --- a \emph{saddle}-structured sparse
matrix. Three families compete:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Direct} (LU factorization: \code{splu} on the CPU,
        \textbf{cuDSS} on the GPU). Robust, tuning-free, exact to
        round-off. But the triangular factors \emph{fill in}: their
        nonzeros grow far faster than $A$'s, cheaply in 2-D and
        punishingly in 3-D, until the factors do not fit in memory.
  \item \textbf{Block-preconditioned iterative} (\textbf{blockch} /
        \code{blockch\_dev}). An outer FGMRES wrapped around a block
        factorization that \emph{never} forms the global LU. It is
        latency-bound at small $n$ (loses badly in 2-D), but its memory
        and per-solve cost scale gently --- so it wins in 3-D past the
        point cuDSS runs out of memory.
  \item \textbf{Matrix-free}. Never stores $A$ at all --- applies the
        action $A\mathbf v$ batch-wise through the element kernels. The
        only survivor once even the sparse $A$ overflows 32-bit indexing
        or the card's memory.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why the choice flips with dimension.} In 2-D the interface is a
curve and the matrix bandwidth is modest, so direct fill-in is cheap ---
cuDSS dominates. In 3-D the interface is a surface, the bandwidth
explodes, and the LU factors blow past the card's memory; that is
exactly where the block preconditioner takes over, and beyond \emph{it},
matrix-free. Solver choice is a function of where on the cost curve your
problem sits.
\end{keybox}

\section{The 2-D crossover, measured live}

We assemble the \emph{real} Cahn--Hilliard Jacobian at three mesh sizes
and time the linear solve (factorize $+$ solve) with CPU-direct
(\code{splu}) and GPU-direct (cuDSS). Isolating the solve matters: the
full step is host-assembly-bound in this brick, which would swamp the
solver signal.

\begin{runbox}
\textbf{Run it.}
\begin{lstlisting}
cd computational/04_solvers
python run.py            # live 2-D benchmark + cited 3-D + the rule
\end{lstlisting}
\end{runbox}

At $\CfourSmallDofs$ dofs, cuDSS is $\sim\CfourSmallSpeedup\times$ the
speed of \code{splu} --- i.e.\ \emph{slower}: the GPU's launch and
transfer overhead dominates a tiny solve. By $\CfourBigDofs$ dofs cuDSS
is $\sim\CfourBigSpeedup\times$ \emph{faster} and pulling away. The
crossover sits near $10^4$ dofs (Fig.~\ref{fig:c4cross}). The block
preconditioner is $26$--$500\times$ slower than cuDSS across this whole
2-D range (dev note, cited) --- you would never use it here.

\section{The 3-D story, measured (and cited)}

The 3-D numbers take minutes per step to produce, so we cite the
development notes' measured tables rather than re-run them. The picture
is decisive (Fig.~\ref{fig:c4cited}):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item cuDSS's factorization fill hits the 48\,GB card at
        $\sim\CfourCudssCeiling$ dofs --- beyond that the factorization
        crawls into hybrid memory and never returns.
  \item \code{blockch\_dev} beats cuDSS $\CfourBlockchSpeedup\times$ on
        the slab64 solve (2.10 vs 17.7 s/call) \emph{and} keeps marching
        at $\CfourCudssCeiling$ dofs, where cuDSS ceilings.
  \item a \emph{block-masked} cuDSS pattern cuts the 3-D fill
        ($17.7\to\CfourMaskedScall$ s/call) --- a strong option under
        $\sim5\times10^5$ dofs, but the memory wall above it is
        unchanged in kind.
  \item \textbf{AMGX verdict: no.} At production $\Delta t$ the inner
        solves are mass-dominated (well-conditioned), so algebraic
        multigrid has no $O(h^{-1})$ growth to flatten and adds nothing.
  \item matrix-free (and \code{blockch}) push the ceiling to
        $\CfourMatrixfreeDofs$ dofs ($128^2\times64$) on a single
        48\,GB card --- a size cuDSS cannot touch on any card here.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The decision, as a rule} (\code{choose\_solver} in the
tutorial):
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
regime & dofs (order) & solver \\
\midrule
2-D, small        & $<10^4$          & \code{splu} (CPU direct) \\
2-D, large        & $>10^4$          & \textbf{cuDSS} (GPU direct) \\
3-D, small        & $<5\times10^5$   & cuDSS / masked cuDSS \\
3-D, large        & up to $\sim1.5\times10^6$ & \code{blockch\_dev} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{keybox}

\section{Code walk-through}

\paragraph{Capturing the real operator.}
\begin{lstlisting}
A, b, dofs, nnz = capture_ch_system(level)   # intercepts the solve
\end{lstlisting}
The benchmark does not invent a matrix --- it runs one real CH Newton
iterate and intercepts the stepper's linear solve to grab the exact
saddle Jacobian $A$ and residual $b$.

\paragraph{The fair timing.}
\begin{lstlisting}
t_splu  = time(lambda: splu(A.tocsc()).solve(b))          # CPU, refactor
t_cudss = time(lambda: solve_linear(A, b, solver="cudss", cache={}))  # GPU
\end{lstlisting}
Both refactor on every call (fresh \code{cache=\{\}}), so the comparison
includes factorization --- the honest cost of a direct solve inside a
Newton loop where $A$ changes each iterate.

dofs). Does cuDSS's lead over \code{splu} keep growing, and does the
solve time scale closer to $O(n)$ or $O(n^{1.5})$? What does the
observed exponent say about 2-D direct fill-in?}

\question{Time \emph{just the solve} versus \emph{factorize $+$ solve} by
reusing one factorization for many right-hand sides. In a Newton loop
$A$ changes every iterate, so you cannot reuse --- but where in this
simulator \emph{could} you (hint: a fixed-point or a frozen-Jacobian
scheme), and how much would it save?}

\question{The dev note reports \code{blockch\_dev} at $8.4\times$ cuDSS
on the slab64 \emph{solve} but only $2.6\times$ on the \emph{step}. What
does the rest of the step cost, and why does the solver speedup not carry
through one-to-one to wall-clock? (Look at the assembly and outer-FGMRES
costs.)}
