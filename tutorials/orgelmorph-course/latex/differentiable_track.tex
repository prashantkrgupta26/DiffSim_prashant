\chapter{What differentiable simulation is}
\label{ch:d1}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} A classical solver answers \emph{``what happens?''}.
A differentiable solver also answers \emph{``what should I change?''} ---
it returns the gradient of any scalar output $J$ with respect to any
input parameter $p$ (material coefficients, a process schedule,
geometry). That single object $dJ/dp$ turns the simulator from a
\emph{predictor} into an \emph{instrument for design and inference}. And
because a gradient you cannot trust is worse than none, DiffSim computes
it \emph{three independent ways} and checks that they agree.
\end{keybox}

\section{Why a gradient}

Every question we ask of a morphology simulator downstream --- fit this
material parameter to a measured morphology, choose a quench schedule
that hits a target crystallinity, learn a free-energy functional from
snapshots --- is an \emph{optimization}. Optimization needs a gradient:
$dJ/dp$ says how a scalar objective $J$ (a domain size, an interfacial
area, a crystalline fraction) moves when you nudge a parameter $p$.

The naive way to get $dJ/dp$ is finite differences: run the simulation
at $p+\epsilon$ and $p-\epsilon$ and subtract. That costs \emph{two
forward solves per parameter} and forces you to tune $\epsilon$ (too big:
truncation error; too small: roundoff). With hundreds of parameters --- a
temperature schedule sampled every step, a free energy with many
coefficients --- it is hopeless. The \textbf{adjoint method} returns
$dJ/dp$ for \emph{all} parameters at once, at the cost of a single extra
(linear) solve backward through the trajectory, exact to machine
precision. That $O(1)$-in-parameters cost is what makes design possible.

\section{The three ways to a gradient}

DiffSim's differentiable core (\file{src/diffsim/adjoint/}) computes the
same gradient three ways, and the tutorial runs all three live:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Adjoint} --- a hand-derived reverse sweep. We do
        \emph{not} tape the Newton iterations; we differentiate only the
        converged discrete relation $R_n(x_n; x_{\text{hist}}, p)=0$ (the
        implicit-function theorem). For $J=\sum_n j(x_n)$,
        %
        \begin{equation}
          J_n^{\!\top}\lambda_n = \dd{j}{x_n}
            - \!\sum_{k\ge1}\Big(\dd{R_{n+k}}{x_n}\Big)^{\!\top}\!\lambda_{n+k},
          \qquad
          \dd{J}{p} = -\sum_n \lambda_n^{\!\top}\,\dd{R_n}{p},
          \label{eq:d1adj}
        \end{equation}
        %
        where $J_n=\partial R_n/\partial x_n$ is \emph{exactly} the
        Jacobian the forward Newton already assembled at convergence, and
        $\partial R_n/\partial p$ is analytic. This is the production
        capability.
  \item \textbf{Autograd twin} --- a faithful PyTorch reimplementation of
        the \emph{same} discrete solver, differentiated by
        \code{torch.autograd} through the converged Newton loop. Different
        code, a different differentiation engine, the same math: an
        \emph{independent} reference.
  \item \textbf{Finite differences} --- central differences
        $\big(J(p+\epsilon)-J(p-\epsilon)\big)/2\epsilon$. No calculus at
        all; the ground truth every gradient must match, down to the FD
        error floor.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The adjoint differentiates the \emph{real} brick.} The discrete
forward in \file{adjoint/phasefield.py} is a vectorised re-implementation
of the production Cahn--Hilliard residual and Jacobian that is
\emph{bit-parity} with \code{CahnHilliardStepper} (measured
$\max|\Delta c|\sim10^{-16}$). So the gradient is taken through the same
operator the research code marches --- not a look-alike toy.
\end{keybox}

\section{Code walk-through}

The tutorial's core is \file{three\_way.py}; it drives
\file{src/diffsim/adjoint/phasefield.py}.

\paragraph{1. One reverse sweep, all gradients.}
\begin{lstlisting}
fwd = CHForward(dm, FHEnergy(A, B), M=M, kappa=kappa, dt=dt, order=order)
fwd.set_initial(ic); fwd.run(n_steps)               # record the trajectory
dJdx = [zeros] * n_steps
dJdx[-1][0::2] = fwd.steps[-1]["c"] - target         # dj/dc on the final step
g_adj = CHAdjoint(fwd).gradient(dJdx, ["M","kappa","B","A"])
\end{lstlisting}
\code{gradient} runs \eqref{eq:d1adj} backward through the recorded
steps and returns \emph{every} $dJ/dp$ from that one sweep.

\paragraph{2. The independent twin.}
\begin{lstlisting}
twin = CHTwin(dm, energy="fh", dt=dt, order=order, device="cpu")
out  = twin.march(c0, None, M, kappa, dict(A=A, B=B), n_steps)  # leaf tensors
loss = 0.5 * ((out[-1][0] - target)**2).sum();  loss.backward()
\end{lstlisting}
Autograd differentiates through the converged Newton iterations; because
the loop is driven to a tight residual, that returns the same
implicit-function-theorem gradient the hand adjoint derives.

\paragraph{3. The ground truth.}
Central differences on each parameter --- two forward solves apiece --- with
no calculus, is what pins the other two to reality.

\chapter{Morphology sensitivity}
\label{ch:d2}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} With the gradient now trustworthy, ask a physical
question: how sensitive is the \emph{morphology} to the material
parameters? Pick a scalar summary of the pattern --- here the
\emph{demixing amplitude} $J=\tfrac12\lVert c_N-\bar c\rVert^2$, the
spatial variance of the composition --- and the adjoint returns
$dJ/d\chi$ and $dJ/d\kappa$ in one sweep. The \emph{signs} are the
physics: $dJ/d\chi>0$ (interaction sharpens the pattern),
$dJ/d\kappa<0$ (gradient penalty blurs it).
\end{keybox}

\section{A scalar handle on the morphology}

A morphology is a whole field; to differentiate we need a scalar. Any
morphology metric will do --- domain size, interfacial area, a structure
factor --- and the adjoint machinery is identical: pick $J(c_N)$, hand it
the seed $dj/dc_N$, get every $dJ/dp$. We use the demixing amplitude
%
\begin{equation}
  J(c_N) = \tfrac12\sum_i\big(c_{N,i}-\bar c\big)^2 ,
  \qquad \bar c=\text{mean}(c_0),
  \label{eq:d2J}
\end{equation}
%
which is small for a nearly uniform blend and grows as the two phases
separate --- a scalar proxy for ``how demixed is the film''. Because the
Cahn--Hilliard dynamics conserve mass, $\bar c$ is fixed by the initial
condition, so the seed
$dj/dc_N = (c_N-\bar c)$ is exact and clean.

The two sensitivities have opposite, and physical, signs. Above the
spinodal ($\chi>2A$) a larger interaction $\chi$ deepens the enthalpic
well and drives demixing harder, so $J$ grows: $dJ/d\chi>0$. The
gradient penalty $\tfrac{\kappa}{2}|\grad c|^2$ instead \emph{opposes}
composition variation --- a larger $\kappa$ thickens interfaces and
smooths the field --- so $dJ/d\kappa<0$. The adjoint recovers both, and
finite differences confirm them.

\chapter{Recovering parameters from a morphology}
\label{ch:d3}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} Turn the arrow around. Instead of ``given the
material, what morphology?'', ask ``given the morphology, what
material?''. This is an \emph{inverse problem}: minimise the misfit
between the simulated and the target field over the material parameters,
using the adjoint gradient. It is the tutorial-scale version of
\emph{learning the material from the morphology} --- the loop that, at
research scale, fits a free-energy functional to measured micrographs.
\end{keybox}

\section{The inverse problem}

Given a target composition field $c_N^{\text{target}}$ (from an
experiment, or --- here --- from a known ground-truth material so we can
check the answer), find the parameters $\theta=(\chi,\kappa)$ that
reproduce it. The objective is the field misfit
%
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta) = \tfrac12\big\lVert c_N(\theta) - c_N^{\text{target}}\big\rVert^2 ,
  \label{eq:d3L}
\end{equation}
%
and we minimise it by quasi-Newton descent (L-BFGS-B). Every optimiser
step needs $L$ \emph{and} its gradient $dL/d\theta$; the adjoint delivers
both from one forward rollout plus one reverse sweep --- the seed is
$dj/dc_N = c_N-c_N^{\text{target}}$, exactly the D2 machinery with a
different scalar on top.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Identifiability.} The recovery works because $\chi$ and $\kappa$
leave \emph{distinguishable} fingerprints: the interaction sets \emph{how
much} the blend demixes, the gradient penalty sets the \emph{interface
width}. When two parameters move the morphology the same way, no gradient
can separate them and the loss stalls on a valley floor --- a real hazard
of inverse problems, and one of the questions below.
\end{keybox}

\section{Code walk-through}

The objective-and-gradient the optimiser calls is the whole method:
\begin{lstlisting}
def loss_and_grad(theta, target):
    fwd, cN = march(dm, coords, chi=theta[0], kappa=theta[1], ...)
    r = cN - target;  L = 0.5 * (r @ r)          # eq. (D3)
    dJdx[-1][0::2] = r                            # dL/dc_N
    g = CHAdjoint(fwd).gradient(dJdx, ["B", "kappa"])
    return L, np.array([g["B"], g["kappa"]])

minimize(loss_and_grad, theta0, jac=True, method="L-BFGS-B", bounds=...)
\end{lstlisting}
\code{scipy}'s L-BFGS-B does the rest. Bounds keep the trial parameters
in the interior region where the Flory--Huggins logs stay smooth --- a
reminder that inverse problems inherit the forward model's domain of
validity.

\chapter{Process gradients}
\label{ch:d4}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} Material parameters are what the blend \emph{is};
\emph{process} parameters are what we \emph{do} to it. The headline
process control is the temperature schedule $T(t)$ --- the quench ---
applied while the film crystallises. The adjoint returns the gradient of
a morphology objective with respect to the \emph{whole schedule at once},
as a \emph{time series} $dJ/dT_n$: one number per step, from a single
reverse sweep. That is what makes designing a process (a long schedule)
tractable.
\end{keybox}

\section{Temperature enters everywhere}

The physics is the coupled Cahn--Hilliard $\times$ Allen--Cahn
crystallisation system (Physics~P6--P7): a conserved composition $\phi$
and a non-conserved crystallinity $\psi$. Temperature enters each step's
residual through \emph{two} channels:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{drive} = \Delta h\,\Big(\tfrac{T}{T_m}-1\Big)
  \quad\text{(Turnbull crystallisation)},
  \qquad
  B(T) = B_0 + b_T\,(T-T_{\text{ref}})
  \quad\text{(Flory interaction)} .
\end{equation}
%
So a change in $T$ at step $n$ ripples through both the crystallisation
thermodynamics and the demixing thermodynamics of that step, and then
forward through every later step via the coupled history. The adjoint
sweep runs that dependency backward and returns $dJ/dT_n$ for all $n$
simultaneously.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{One sweep, $N$ gradients.} A finite-difference schedule gradient
costs $2N$ extra forward solves (perturb each $T_n$ up and down); the
adjoint costs \emph{one} backward solve, independent of the schedule
length $N$. For a schedule sampled every time step --- hundreds of
controls --- only the adjoint is affordable. This is the same $O(1)$-in-
parameters advantage as D1, now paying off on a genuinely high-
dimensional control.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Crystallinity sensitivity}
\label{ch:d5}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} Device performance often hinges on \emph{crystallinity}
--- how much of the film has ordered. This concept differentiates a
crystallinity metric through the coupled Cahn--Hilliard $\times$
Allen--Cahn rollout with respect to the thermodynamic crystallisation
parameters ($\Delta h,T_m,\Delta\sigma,\varepsilon^2,L$) --- all five from
one adjoint sweep, each finite-difference verified. Now the sensitivities
flow through the \emph{non-conserved} crystallinity field $\psi$, not just
the composition.
\end{keybox}

\section{Differentiating the crystalline order}

The crystallisation free energy (Physics~P6) adds to the demixing energy
a driven Allen--Cahn field $\psi$:
%
\begin{equation}
  f = f_{\rm FH}(\phi) + \phi\big[q(\psi)\,\Delta\sigma
      + p(\psi)\,\text{drive}\big],
  \qquad \text{drive} = \Delta h\Big(\tfrac{T}{T_m}-1\Big),
\end{equation}
%
with $q(\psi)=\psi^2(1-\psi)^2$ the double-well barrier (height
$\Delta\sigma$) and $p(\psi)=\psi^2(3-2\psi)$ the monotone interpolation.
The crystallinity relaxes by $\dot\psi=-L\,[\,\partial f/\partial\psi -
\varepsilon^2\nabla^2\psi\,]$. Five parameters shape it: the latent heat
$\Delta h$ and melting point $T_m$ set the \emph{driving force}, the
barrier $\Delta\sigma$ \emph{opposes} ordering, the gradient penalty
$\varepsilon^2$ sets \emph{interface stiffness}, and $L$ sets the
\emph{rate}. We take a crystallinity metric $J=\tfrac12\lVert\psi_N-
\psi^\star\rVert^2$ and let the adjoint report how each moves it.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Reading the signs.} At the tutorial setting the mean crystallinity
$\langle\psi\rangle\approx\DfiveFrac$ sits \emph{below} the target
$\psi^\star=\DfivePsiTgt$. So any parameter that \emph{raises}
crystallinity lowers $J$: raising the latent heat $\Delta h$ deepens the
drive ($dJ/d\Delta h<0$), while raising the barrier $\Delta\sigma$
resists ordering ($dJ/d\Delta\sigma>0$). The gradient's sign is a
statement of physics, and it is exactly what finite differences confirm.
\end{keybox}

\section{A frontier, named honestly}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Noise is a different derivative.} Nucleation is seeded by a

\chapter{Learning the free energy from snapshots}
\label{ch:d6}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} D3 recovered scalar \emph{parameters} of a known free
energy. Now recover the free-energy \emph{functional} itself: given a time
series of morphology snapshots, learn the bulk energy $f(c)$ that produced
them. This is \emph{learning the thermodynamics from the morphology} ---
the Wodo--Ganapathysubramanian programme --- at tutorial scale, and it runs
on exactly the same adjoint.
\end{keybox}

\section{A learnable free energy}

Parametrise the derivative of the bulk energy on a monomial basis,
%
\begin{equation}
  f'(c) = \sum_i a_i\,c^{p_i},
  \qquad p = [3,\,1],
  \qquad \dd{f'}{a_i} = c^{p_i},
  \label{eq:d6basis}
\end{equation}
%
so the coefficient sensitivity $\partial f'/\partial a_i$ is just the
basis function --- it drops straight into the adjoint's $\partial
R_\mu/\partial p = -\!\int N\,(\partial f'/\partial p)$. The ground truth
is the canonical double well $f'(c)=c^3-c$, i.e. $a=[1,-1]$. We fit the
$a_i$ by minimising the misfit over a \emph{trajectory} of snapshots,
%
\begin{equation}
  L(a) = \sum_{n\in\mathcal S}\tfrac12\big\lVert c_n(a)-c_n^{\text{data}}
  \big\rVert^2 ,
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathcal S$ a chosen subset of steps; the gradient $dL/da$ comes
from one CHAdjoint sweep that seeds every snapshot step.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{What a morphology cannot tell you.} A constant term in $f'(c)$
merely shifts the chemical potential $\mu$ by a constant; the conserved
$c$-dynamics ($c_t=\grad\!\cdot M\grad\mu$) are invariant to it. So a
constant is \emph{unidentifiable} from composition data, and the basis
\eqref{eq:d6basis} excludes it on purpose. Knowing what your data
\emph{cannot} constrain is as important as the fit itself.
\end{keybox}

\chapter{Capstone: inverse-designing a process}
\label{ch:d7}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Key idea.} The capstone closes the loop. Everything before this
\emph{computed} a gradient; here we \emph{use} it to design. This is
PDE-constrained optimisation: find a temperature schedule $T(t)$ that
drives the crystallisation to a \emph{target crystalline fraction}, by
minimising an objective over the whole schedule with the D4 schedule
gradient. The simulator, run backward, returns the recipe.
\end{keybox}

\section{Design as optimisation}

Specify what you want --- here a target crystalline fraction
$\phi^\star$ --- as an objective over the process control $T(t)$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \min_{T\in[T_{\min},T_{\max}]^N}\ J(T) = \tfrac12\big(\langle\psi_N
  \rangle(T) - \phi^\star\big)^2 ,
  \label{eq:d7}
\end{equation}
%
where $\langle\psi_N\rangle$ is the mean crystallinity the schedule
produces. Each optimiser step needs $J$ and its gradient $dJ/dT_n$; the
adjoint returns the whole time-series gradient (D4) from one reverse
sweep, and a bounded quasi-Newton method (L-BFGS-B) walks the schedule
downhill. The bounds keep the temperatures physical.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A reachable target, so we can check.} The target is generated by a
ground-truth cold ramp, so a schedule that hits it provably exists ---
which lets the tutorial \emph{verify} that the designed process reaches
the specification, not merely that the optimiser stopped. Point the same
machinery at a real specification and it returns a candidate recipe.
\end{keybox}

\section{Run it}

\section{Code walk-through}

The objective-and-gradient is the D4 schedule gradient wrapped in an
optimiser:
\begin{lstlisting}
def obj_and_grad(T, target):
    f = march(dm, coords, T, dt)                 # coupled crystallisation
    m = mean(f.steps[-1]["x"][2::3])             # <psi_N>
    J = 0.5 * (m - target)**2
    dJ[-1][2::3] = (m - target) / nn             # d<psi_N>/dpsi_i
    return J, CACHAdjoint(f).temperature_gradient(dJ)

minimize(obj_and_grad, T0, jac=True, method="L-BFGS-B", bounds=[(0.2,2.0)]*NS)
\end{lstlisting}
That is the whole capstone: a forward crystallisation, a reverse sweep for
the schedule gradient, and \code{scipy} to descend. Every earlier concept
--- the verified gradient (D1), the sensitivities (D2, D4, D5), the inverse
fits (D3, D6) --- was a rehearsal for this.
